Welcome back to Saving the Planet, our weekly programme about nature and conservation.
This evening we're focusing on Australia's Great Barrier Reef.
To talk about what's happening to the reef, we have invited a member of the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority.
Welcome, Gordon Kelly.
Thank you. Good to be here.
So, Gordon, tell us how serious the situation is.
The situation is dramatic.
The coral reef is particularly sensitive to the effects of climate change
and has suffered a record number of mass bleaching events in recent years.
In fact, the temperatures last December, which is of course our summer, were the highest on record.
There were alarming levels of heat, putting the reef at risk of a mass bleaching of corals.
Can you explain what the bleaching effect is?
Corals are living animals and get most of their food and colour from the algae that live within them.
But if temperatures rise, the algae separate and leave the coral bleached white.
There have been five mass bleaching events since 2016, all because of rising ocean temperatures caused by global heating.
So this is connected to climate change?
Undoubtedly.
Does the bleaching kill the coral?
Corals can recover from mild bleaching, but are weaker, more vulnerable to disease, and reproduce less in the following years.
In the worst affected areas, most of the shallow water corals are dying.
Recovery can be helped by weather conditions.
Cloud cover, wind and rainfall all act to reduce temperatures.
But we all know that global temperatures are rising, not falling.
The situation is urgent.
The Australian government recently announced that warming waters from escalating climate change
have caused coral bleaching in 91% of the reefs along the Great Barrier Reef this year alone.
Scientists say there is little time left for the reefs to recover,
and that if governments don't address the climate crisis soon, it may be too late.
