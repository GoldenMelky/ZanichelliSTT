John. When John was running for a seat in Parliament during the last election,
he discovered that many young people in his district were voting for the first time.
I wish I could meet them all so that we could have a chance to talk to each other, he thought.
So he decided to speak at every college and university, and most of the secondary schools as well.
The students he met were deeply interested in social issues, such as freedom of expression and civil rights.
Abigail. When Abigail Cumming-Vukovic was 16, she launched an initiative in her school in Columbus, Ohio,
to encourage girls to participate in politics.
I remember being 8 years old and looking at a poster of all the American presidents,
and wondering why there wasn't a woman among them, she said to her mates.
If only it were possible for one of us to enter politics, be elected, and become the first female president.
Abigail represents a new wave of young leaders.
Girls who have already decided, before some of them can even vote,
that they will put themselves forward as candidates, support one another, and persuade people to vote for them.
Annie and Josh. In 2016, the Brexit referendum was held to decide if the United Kingdom should stay in the European Union.
About 64% of the young citizens who were registered voted, and about 75% of them voted to remain in the EU.
Annie and Josh, who were 17 at the time, wished they had been able to vote.
They wished the voting age were lowered to 16 in the UK.
Keir. When Keir Mather was 25, he became the baby of the House.
That is, the youngest member of the British Parliament.
He threw himself into his parliamentary duties, saying he would never have the time for another job.
He sees himself as a young fighter.
I wish he inspired other young people to get into politics, one of his voters said.
